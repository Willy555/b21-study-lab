Choosing between for and to
When we switch the order of objects in a sentence, the
choice between for and to can depend on the verb. Certain
verbs typically pair with only for or to, while others allow
either option. Sometimes, this choice alters the meaning.

Use for + indirect object with verbs like build, find, and create.

Please find me a flat with two bedrooms in the city centre.
Please find a flat with two bedrooms in the city centre
for me.

Use to + indirect object with verbs like give, offer, and show.

She showed the customer the brochure.
She showed the brochure to the customer.

Either can be used with verbs like read, write, and explain.
The choice between them can change the meaning of
the sentence.
I wrote a WhatsApp message to my mum. (My mum was
the receiver.)
| wrote a WhatsApp message for my mum. (My mum was
the sender, and | helped her.)

Other times, the meaning remains very similar.
The teacher read the instructions to/for the students.

Certain verbs with two objects cannot switch their objects

Fractions

Fractions are a way to express parts of a whole. The numerator
(top number or first number) shows the number of parts,
while the denominator (bottom number or second number)
shows the total number of equal parts that something has
been divided into.

Two thirds of the community in Okinawa actively engage
in daily physical activities such as walking and gardening.

In this example, two is the numerator. The numerator

is always a cardinal number (e.g. one, two, three). The
denominator is an ordinal number (e.g. third, fifth, eighth).
Note that if the denominator is a 2, we say half, and if it’s a
4 we say quarter.

Expressions with fractions
We can use fractions to compare quantities, rates, or extents,
and emphasize the difference between them.

the amount of processed food in typical Western diets.
(quantity)

compared to national averages, probably due to their
healthier lifestyles. (rate)

spend a tenth as much time in front of screens as in
other areas. (extent)

Using a or one with fractions

A and one can usually be used interchangeably, but one is

more emphatic.

as their ancestors.

Indirect questions

Indirect questions are used to sound more polite, or to report
another person's question at a different place or time. Unlike
direct questions, we don’t use auxiliary verbs before the
subject, and we use the same word order as in statements.

Could you tell me what the time is, please?
Do you know where I can find a cash machine near here?

I'd be grateful if you could let me know if you will be in the
office later.

We use if or whether in indirect questions that don’t have a
wh- question word in them (what, where, when, how, why,
etc.). A question mark is not usually used.

I'd like to know if this year’s festival will include public
screenings.

| was wondering whether you have seen the latest episode.
It’s asking whether you want to rent the film or buy it.

However, in informal contexts, a question mark might be
added to show that the writer is expecting a response.

| was wondering if you would like to have dinner with
me tonight?

Subject questions & object questions

Subject questions have the question word as the subject of
the sentence.

Who directed the film?
Steven Spielberg directed it.

In subject questions, we use the same verb forms as we

would use in a positive statement, and we don’t change the
word order.

What happened at the end of the episode?
How many people went to the cinema last weekend?

Object questions have the question word as the object of
the sentence.

What did Steven Spielberg direct?

He directed E.T., Jaws, Schindler’s List, and /ots of
other films.

In object questions, we use the question form of the verb and
question word order.

Where did you go after the film?
Who does the heroine end up marrying in the end?

Declarative questions

In a declarative question, the sentence takes the form of a
statement but is intended as a question. They are typically
used to check understanding, express surprise, or confirm
information when we already have some knowledge about a
person or situation.

You’re going by train?
You seriously enjoyed that film?
Oh no! She’s already seen it?

Declarative questions are used more often in speaking than
writing. These questions usually feature rising intonation,
signalling that the statement is actually a question.

Comparative structures

There are lots of grammatical patterns to compare things in
English in addition to the basic comparatives and superlatives
you already know.

Adjective + enough + infinitive
The local government isn’t equipped enough to manage
the yearly flooding effectively.

Alot + comparative
Efficient water purification technologies are a lot more
affordable than many governments assume.

As if / as though + finite clause (to introduce a comparison)
The policy treats water scarcity as though it were a minor
issue, when it is a critical concern.

Much + comparative + noun
Clean drinking water is a much higher priority in rural
areas than other infrastructure projects.

Rather than + verb-ing (to introduce a comparison)
Rather than being ignored, the issues of water leakage
and waste should be addressed immediately.

The + comparative ... the + comparative
The more efficient our water use, the greater the
improvement in overall community health.

Too + adjective + a/an + noun
It’s too significant a challenge for one organization to
address alone; it will take a collaborative effort.

Cleft sentences with it

Cleft sentences emphasize a particular part of a sentence by
restructuring it.

Cleft sentences with it start with it followed by a verb
(typically a form of be) and the focal element. The rest of the
sentence is then introduced by a relative item (e.g. that) and
normally contains information that is already understood.

It is the overfishing of certain species that has led to
imbalances in marine ecosystems.

It’s this kind of environmental work where the real
difference is being made.

It'll be the next century when we really get to see if we've
done enough to protect our waterways.

Using different pronouns and determiners in cleft sentences
with it allows us to place additional emphasis on the focus of
the sentence.

It was all the community’s efforts that restored the
coral reef.

It was those species most vulnerable to climate change
that showed the first signs of population decline.

It was that documentary on ocean pollution that sparked
widespread public outcry.
